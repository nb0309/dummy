# WCAG 1.3.3 Sensory Characteristics — design notes

Why a criterion that fails in prose still needs a probe, what the lexicon can and cannot
do, and the two bugs the fixtures caught. Mechanics live in
[`generator/README.md`](../generator/README.md). Fourth in the series after
[`2.1.2`](2.1.2-no-keyboard-trap.md), [`3.2.1`](3.2.1-on-focus.md) and
[`3.2.2`](3.2.2-on-input.md).

## The odd one out

**1.3.3 (Level A):** instructions for understanding and operating content must not rely
**solely** on sensory characteristics — shape, colour, size, visual location,
orientation, or sound.

Everything built here before it fails in one of two places:

| | fails in | detected by |
|---|---|---|
| 2.1.2, 3.2.1, 3.2.2 | behaviour | driving the page and watching |
| 2.4.3, 2.4.4, 2.4.6, 1.3.2 | structure | reading the tree and the geometry |
| **1.3.3** | **language** | reading the prose — and there is no way to do that mechanically |

"Click the round button on the right" is a defect. "Click the round Submit button on the
right" is not. The markup is byte-identical; one word of prose is the entire difference.

Two consequences follow immediately. The sample must be **page-level**, because an
instruction and its referent are different elements and often far apart — the
per-element sweep would capture the button and drop the sentence that fails. And the
probe's job is unusually **narrow**, because most of what this criterion needs is
already in the evidence.

## Why a probe at all, when the model can read the page

With a `<body>` sample the model already receives the prose (`element_html`), the
components (same), and the stylesheet (`parent_html`'s `<head>`). It was a real question
whether anything else was needed. Three things justified it:

1. **Resolved layout geometry.** What is actually left/right/above/below is the product
   of flex, grid, float and absolute positioning. A stylesheet states *causes*, not
   positions. `instruction-refers-to-position-only.html` exists to make this concrete:
   `flex-direction: row-reverse` puts the links the instruction calls "on the right"
   **first** in source order. They measure at `x=1044` against a midline of `640`. No
   amount of reading the CSS yields that without simulating layout.
2. **Computed colour.** `class="go"` means nothing until the cascade runs.
   `rgb(0, 112, 60)` → green is a resolution, not a lookup.
3. **`namesInSentence`.** Derivable in principle — but it is the decisive test, and the
   probe already knows every component's real accessible name, so checking whether one
   appears in the sensory sentence is free.

Everything else the probe could have reported would have been restatement, and it does
not report it.

## The lexicon is crude and the design says so

Detection is a word list. It has to be, and it is wrong constantly:

- "You have the **right** to appeal" — an entitlement.
- "See **below** for our address" — a section, not a control.
- "A **large** number of appeals" — a quantity.
- "**Green** belt land" — a proper noun. "In the **round**" — an idiom.

`position-word-is-not-an-instruction.html` is built entirely from these and is a
**pass**: four candidates, no instruction among them, no component identified. It is the
1.3.3 equivalent of 2.4.6's `repeated-link-text-same-destination` — a fixture whose only
job is to stop the mechanism firing on ordinary content.

So the evidence never resolves a candidate to a verdict. It makes the model answer two
questions in order, and expects most candidates to die at the first:

1. Is this sentence an **instruction**, and does it identify a component at all?
2. Only then: is the sensory characteristic the **sole** identifier?

That second question is the criterion, and it is worth stating what it does *not* say.
Mentioning colour is not the defect. "The green Confirm button" is exemplary — the
colour serves the people who can see it and the name serves everyone else.
`required-fields-marked-by-colour-and-text.html` keeps its red labels for exactly this
reason: removing colour would help nobody.

## Two bugs the fixtures caught

### `"\b"` in a JavaScript string is a backspace

The first matcher used `lower.includes(word)`, so **"red" matched inside "requi*red*"
and "conside*red*"** — inventing colour references on two pages that contain none, one
of them the fixture that exists to prove ordinary prose is not a finding.

The fix was word-boundary matching, and the fix had a bug of its own:

```js
new RegExp("\b" + word + "\b")   // \b in a string literal is U+0008, BACKSPACE
```

This matches nothing. It also fails *silently and partially*: multi-word entries like
"on the right" use `indexOf` and kept working, so the capture still produced references
and looked merely under-tuned rather than broken. Only single-word entries — which is
most of the lexicon — had stopped firing. The tell was that every colour, shape and size
match vanished at once while position survived.

```js
new RegExp(String.raw`\b${word}\b`)   // correct
```

`String.raw` is used rather than `"\\b"` specifically because the escaped spelling is the
one that invites the mistake a second time.

### Landmarks are referents too

`candidates` originally held interactive elements and `<label>`s. But
`instruction-names-region-and-position.html` says "use the **Refine results** panel on
the right", and the panel is a `<nav aria-label="Refine results">` — not a control, so
not in the list, so `namesInSentence` came back `[]` and a **conforming** page produced
the same decisive signal as the failing one.

Named regions and headings are now included. The field is called `candidates` rather
than `controls` for that reason: what an instruction points at is not always something
you can operate.

## What the pair proves

`instruction-names-button-by-colour` and `instruction-names-button-and-colour` are the
same page — same two buttons, same computed colours, same layout, same sentence shape:

```text
fail: "press the green button to submit"          namesInSentence: []
pass: "press the green Confirm button to submit"  namesInSentence: ["Confirm"]
```

One word. That is the criterion, and it is why `namesInSentence` is captured rather than
left to inference.

A known imprecision, visible in the same fixture: "Press the red button to **discard**
it" reports `namesInSentence: ["Discard"]` because the word matches a button of that
name — but it is a verb, not a reference. The evidence flags this explicitly ("check it
is not a coincidence of wording") rather than pretending the check is exact.

## The 1.4.1 boundary

Information conveyed by colour **with no instruction involved** is 1.4.1 Use of Color,
not 1.3.3 — a status shown only as a red dot, a required field marked only by colour
with no sentence about it. The two overlap constantly and can both apply to one page;
`required-fields-marked-by-colour-only.html` is arguably both. `"1.4.1"` was added to
`_KNOWN_SC` so the skill can report it under its own key, the way 2.4.6 reports link
findings under 2.4.4.

## Status

Mechanically complete. Every fail fixture produces at least one reference in its intended
category; the two resolvable categories resolve against measurement; the false-candidate
fixture produces four candidates and no finding; captures are byte-identical across runs.

**Unverified:** the model's own accuracy. `python -m src.run --input 1.3.3_dataset.csv`
needs `AZURE_OPENAI_ENDPOINT`, unset here as for the last three criteria.

This is the criterion most likely to produce a false positive, and unusually the risk is
on *four* of the five passes rather than one — every pass except the baseline contains
lexicon matches, and `position-word-is-not-an-instruction` contains nothing but. A model
that treats a match as a finding fails them all.
